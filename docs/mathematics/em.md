# P16 — Expectation–maximisation

> With missing values the likelihood no longer splits into one closed-form problem per CPD column,
> because each incomplete row sums over its completions. EM restores the closed form iteratively.
> The **E-step** replaces the unknown counts by their expectations under the current model, and
> the **M-step** solves the complete-data problem (P14, P15) with those expected counts. Each
> iteration maximises a lower bound on the log-likelihood that touches it at the current
> parameters, so the log-likelihood never decreases.

Prerequisites: P12 (junction tree), P14 (likelihood and MLE), P15 (Dirichlet estimation).

---

## 1. Setup

Row $m$ splits into its observed values $o_m$ and its missing values $Z_m$. The **observed-data
log-likelihood** is
$$\ell(\theta)=\sum_m\log P(o_m\mid\theta)=\sum_m\log\sum_{z}P(o_m,Z_m{=}z\mid\theta).$$
This is what `log_likelihood` computes (P14 §7). The sum inside the logarithm couples the CPD
columns, so maximising $\ell$ has no closed form in general.

## 2. Why the missingness can be ignored (MAR)

Let $R$ be the pattern of which values are missing, generated by a mechanism
$P(R\mid x,\phi)$ with its own parameters $\phi$. Then the data are really $(o,R)$, and
$$P(o,R\mid\theta,\phi)=\sum_zP(o,z\mid\theta)\,P(R\mid o,z,\phi).$$
**Missing at random (MAR)** means $P(R\mid o,z,\phi)=P(R\mid o,\phi)$: the chance that a value is
missing may depend on what was observed, but not on what is missing. Then that factor leaves the
sum, and
$$P(o,R\mid\theta,\phi)=P(R\mid o,\phi)\cdot P(o\mid\theta).$$
If $\theta$ and $\phi$ are unrelated, the first factor is a constant as far as $\theta$ is
concerned, so maximising $P(o\mid\theta)$ is exactly right. MCAR (missingness independent of
everything, as in `Dataset.with_missing`) is a special case. Under MNAR, for example when people
with high incomes skip the income question, this argument fails and EM is biased. The library
assumes MAR (spec ⚑7).

## 3. A lower bound (Jensen)

For one row and any distribution $q$ over its completions $z$,
$$\log P(o\mid\theta)=\log\sum_zq(z)\frac{P(o,z\mid\theta)}{q(z)}
\;\ge\;\sum_zq(z)\log\frac{P(o,z\mid\theta)}{q(z)}
=\mathbb E_q[\log P(o,Z\mid\theta)]+H(q),$$
because $\log$ is concave. The gap is exactly
$$\log P(o\mid\theta)-\Big(\mathbb E_q[\log P(o,Z\mid\theta)]+H(q)\Big)=\mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\big\|\,P(Z\mid o,\theta)\big)\ge0,$$
so the bound is **tight** exactly when $q(z)=P(z\mid o,\theta)$.

## 4. The two steps

Start from $\theta^{(t)}$.

**E-step.** For every row, set $q_m=P(Z_m\mid o_m,\theta^{(t)})$. This makes the bound tight at
$\theta^{(t)}$.

**M-step.** Maximise the bound over $\theta$ with the $q_m$ fixed. Since $H(q_m)$ does not depend
on $\theta$, that means maximising
$$Q(\theta\mid\theta^{(t)})=\sum_m\mathbb E_{q_m}\big[\log P(o_m,Z_m\mid\theta)\big]
=\sum_i\sum_u\sum_x\bar N(x,u)\log\theta_{x\mid u},$$
where the **expected counts** are
$$\bar N(x,u)=\sum_mP\big(X_i{=}x,\mathrm{Pa}_i{=}u\mid o_m,\theta^{(t)}\big).$$
The second form follows because the complete-data log-likelihood is
$\sum_{i,u,x}N(x,u)\log\theta_{x\mid u}$ (P14 §4), which is linear in the counts, so its
expectation is the same expression with expected counts. $Q$ has exactly the form of a
complete-data log-likelihood, so by P14 Theorem 2 its maximiser is
$$\theta^{(t+1)}_{x\mid u}=\frac{\bar N(x,u)}{\bar N(u)}.$$

**Computing the expected counts.** Row $m$ needs the joint posterior of each family
$F_i=\{X_i\}\cup\mathrm{Pa}_i$ given $o_m$. If every variable in $F_i$ is observed, the row adds 1
to one cell. Otherwise the hidden part $H=F_i\setminus O_m$ is needed jointly. A family is a clique
of the moral graph, and the junction tree is built from the moral graph with the observed variables
removed, so $H$ is still a clique there and lies inside one clique of the tree (P12, M3 J6). One
calibration per row therefore gives every family's posterior via `JunctionTree.query(H)`. The row
adds $P(H{=}h\mid o_m)$ to the cell that combines $h$ with the row's observed values. Rows with the
same observed values have the same posterior, so they share one calibration and contribute it
times their multiplicity (spec ⚑5).

## 5. Monotonicity

**Theorem 1 (M2).** $\ell(\theta^{(t+1)})\ge\ell(\theta^{(t)})$.

*Proof.* Write $\mathcal F(q,\theta)=\sum_m\big(\mathbb E_{q_m}[\log P(o_m,Z_m\mid\theta)]+H(q_m)\big)$.
By §3, $\ell(\theta)\ge\mathcal F(q,\theta)$ for every $q$ and $\theta$, with equality when $q$ is the
posterior under $\theta$. Then
$$\ell(\theta^{(t+1)})\;\ge\;\mathcal F(q^{(t)},\theta^{(t+1)})\;\ge\;\mathcal F(q^{(t)},\theta^{(t)})\;=\;\ell(\theta^{(t)}).$$
The first inequality is the bound, the second holds because the M-step maximises over $\theta$,
and the equality holds because the E-step made the bound tight. $\square$

EM is coordinate ascent on $\mathcal F$: the E-step maximises over $q$, the M-step over $\theta$.
An increasing sequence that is bounded above ($\ell\le0$) converges, so the log-likelihoods
converge. That does **not** mean the parameters converge to the global maximum. §8 shows a fixed
point that is not even a local maximum.

## 6. Fixed points are stationary points

Suppose $\theta^*$ is a fixed point: $\theta^*$ maximises $Q(\cdot\mid\theta^*)$. The gap
$g(\theta)=\ell(\theta)-\mathcal F(q^*,\theta)$, with $q^*$ the posterior under $\theta^*$, is a KL
divergence. It is $\ge0$ everywhere and $=0$ at $\theta^*$, so it is minimised there and its
gradient (along the simplex) vanishes. Hence $\nabla\ell(\theta^*)=\nabla_\theta Q(\theta\mid\theta^*)|_{\theta^*}$,
which is zero along the simplex because $\theta^*$ maximises $Q$. So **every fixed point of EM is a
stationary point of $\ell$**. It may be a maximum, a saddle point, or (rarely) a minimum.

## 7. Two special cases

**Complete data (M3).** No values are missing, so the posterior of each row is a point mass, the
expected counts are the actual counts whatever $\theta^{(t)}$ is, and the first M-step gives the
MLE. The second iteration would compute the same counts again, so EM stops after one iteration.

**With a Dirichlet prior.** Add $\log p(\theta)=\sum\,(\alpha_{x\mid u}-1)\log\theta_{x\mid u}+\text{const}$
to both sides of §5. The M-step now maximises $Q+\log p$, which has the form of P15's posterior
with counts $\bar N$. Its maximiser is the **MAP** $(\bar N+\alpha-1)/(\bar N(u)+\alpha_{\cdot\mid u}-|X|)$,
and the argument of §5 shows that the **log-posterior** $\ell(\theta)+\log p(\theta)$ never decreases.

The library's default M-step with a prior is the posterior **mean**
$(\bar N+\alpha)/(\bar N(u)+\alpha_{\cdot\mid u})$. That is exactly the MAP under the prior with
pseudocounts $\alpha+1$. So mean-EM is MAP-EM for $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha+1)$, and it never decreases
$\ell(\theta)+\sum(\alpha_{x\mid u})\log\theta_{x\mid u}$. In both cases `EMResult.log_objective`
records the quantity that is guaranteed not to decrease. It is $\ell$ itself without a prior, and
EM stops when it changes by less than `tolerance` (spec ⚑6).

## 8. The symmetric fixed point (M5)

Take a latent-class model: a hidden class $C$ with children $F_1,\ldots,F_k$, and $C$ never
observed. Suppose that at $\theta^{(t)}$, $P(F_j\mid C{=}c)$ is the same for every class $c$.

**Theorem 2.** Then $\theta^{(t+1)}$ has the same property, with the same class prior.

*Proof.* With equal conditionals, $P(o_m\mid C{=}c)$ is the same for every $c$, so the E-step
posterior $P(C\mid o_m)$ equals the prior $\pi=P(C)$ for every row. Write $N$ for the number of
rows, $N_j(f)$ for the rows with $F_j=f$ observed, and $M_j$ for the rows where $F_j$ is missing.
A row with $F_j=f$ observed adds $\pi_c$ to cell $(f,c)$. A row with $F_j$ missing adds
$P(F_j{=}f,C{=}c\mid o_m)=\pi_c\,\theta^{(t)}_f$, where $\theta^{(t)}_f$ is the shared conditional.
So
$$\bar N(c)=\pi_c\,N,\qquad\bar N(f,c)=\pi_c\big(N_j(f)+M_j\,\theta^{(t)}_f\big).$$
The M-step gives $P(C{=}c)=\pi_c$ and
$$P(F_j{=}f\mid C{=}c)=\frac{\pi_c\big(N_j(f)+M_j\theta^{(t)}_f\big)}{\pi_c\,N}=\frac{N_j(f)+M_j\,\theta^{(t)}_f}{N},$$
which is the same for every $c$. $\square$

Within the symmetric set, the iteration $\theta\mapsto(N_j(f)+M_j\theta)/N$ is a contraction with
rate $M_j/N$, so it converges geometrically to its fixed point $\theta_f=N_j(f)/N_j$: the observed
frequencies of $F_j$. When nothing but $C$ is missing, it gets there in one step. At that point
the model fits the features as if they were independent, with log-likelihood
$\sum_j\sum_fN_j(f)\log\big(N_j(f)/N_j\big)$.

So EM started symmetrically never separates the classes, whatever the data say. The point is a
fixed point, hence stationary (§6), but typically a saddle point. Any asymmetric starting point
moves off it. This is why the default initialisation is random, and why the result of EM depends
on its starting point: EM finds a **local** optimum.

## 9. How the tests check this independently

- **Fixture F3**, exactly: one iteration from F1's MLE gives 23/48, 18/23 and 1/5, and $\ell$ rises
  from −13.515406 to −13.314771.
- **Expected counts against brute force (M1):** for small random models and data, sum
  $P(\text{completion}\mid o_m)$ over every completion of every row, using only the joint table.
- **Monotonicity (M2)** on every run in the tests, to within $10^{-10}$, with and without priors.
- **Complete data (M3):** EM equals `maximum_likelihood` (or `bayesian_estimate`) and stops after
  one iteration.
- **Brute-force EM (M7):** an independent EM built from joint tables alone (expected counts by
  enumerating completions, the M-step by array division) agrees iteration by iteration, with and
  without priors, for MAP and mean.
- **The symmetric fixed point (M5):** one step matches Theorem 2's formula exactly; runs from a
  symmetric start stay symmetric and reach $N_j(f)/N_j$ with the independence log-likelihood; a
  $10^{-3}$ nudge escapes to a log-likelihood about 200 higher.
- **Label switching:** random starts reach the same optimum under either labelling of the hidden
  class, so the parameters are identifiable only up to relabelling.
- **MCAR recovery (M6):** on 20,000 rows with 30% of values missing, every EM estimate lies within
  the Bernstein bound for the rows where its family is fully observed, and EM's squared error is
  under half that of the MLE on complete rows only (17% of the data).
- **MAR versus MNAR (§2):** hiding Traffic depending on Rain (MAR), EM recovers $P(T)=0.39$ while
  the average of the observed values is badly biased; hiding it depending on Traffic itself (MNAR),
  EM is biased too.
